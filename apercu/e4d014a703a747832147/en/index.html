How to make money on Instagram in 2026: real payouts, and why 98% of viral Reels sell nothing

By Antonin Girardeau, founder of Prooven · Published October 30, 2026 · 8 min read

In 2026, you make money on Instagram through brand deals, your own offer, subscriptions (18+, over 10,000 followers) and Gifts on Reels ($0.01 per star). Instagram does not pay for views unless it invites you, and US creators need 5 million views a month for three months to qualify. Of nearly 370 viral Reels I classified one by one, 98% exist to get discovered and 0.6% sell anything: the money comes after the view.

In short
  • Instagram's view bonuses are invite-only. The published US bar is 5 million views a month, three months in a row.
  • Subscriptions need over 10,000 followers. Meta takes no cut, but Apple and Google keep about 30% of in-app sign-ups.
  • Across nearly 500 viral Reels, the ones ending with "comment a word and I'll DM you the link" get 10 times more comments per 1,000 views.
  • Only 2.7% of viral English-language Reels use that ending, against 10% of French ones. US creators leave it on the table.
  • The FTC wants paid posts labeled in the video itself, with plain words like "ad" or "sponsored".

Every way Instagram pays creators in 2026

There are seven income sources, and only two have no ceiling: brands and whatever you sell yourself. Everything else hangs on a follower threshold, an invite or your viewers' tips. Here is what's open to US creators and what each one pays.

Instagram won't pay you for views

A Reel with 1 million views earns $0 unless Instagram has invited you to its bonus program. The official page calls bonuses "limited-time" and "invite-only". Even the invite comes with a bar: 5 million views a month in the US, held for three months.

Most creators never see that bar. One in five viral Reels I analyzed clears 5 million views on its own, and that's a single Reel, not a monthly total. I ran the full numbers, including what the same views would earn on TikTok and YouTube Shorts, in does Instagram pay for views. If you want per-view money, Instagram is the wrong app.

98% of viral Reels get you discovered, 0.6% sell

Almost no viral Reel sells something directly. Out of nearly 370 viral Reels whose funnel role I classified, 97.8% are discovery content: a tip, a story, a take. 1.7% nudge toward a purchase. 0.6% pitch a product, and both of those are electric scooter videos.

So your viral Reel brings strangers to your profile. The money shows up one step later: a DM, a link, a sales page. Turn your Reels into thinly veiled ads and you lose the very views that would have found you customers.

"Comment a word": the ending that turns views into leads

The most common bridge from a view to a sale is one closing line: "comment this word and I'll send you the link." An automated DM tool then replies to every comment. Of nearly 500 viral Reels, 17 end this way, or 3.4%.

Those Reels pull 55 comments per 10,000 views. Reels with no call to action get 5.5, and Reels that only ask for a follow or a like get 3.5. The middle comment-a-word Reel gets 10 times the comments, and 88% of them land above the top quarter of every other Reel.

Every comment is someone raising a hand. Jun Yuh, a content strategist with 5.2 million followers, posted a 14-second Reel on October 23, 2025. It got 362,806 views and 2,546 comments, or 7 per 1,000 views, about 13 times the usual rate. The whole pitch fits in the last line:

"Comment 'content' and I'll show you how to do this everyday."

Jun Yuh, Reel from October 23, 2025

Only 2.7% of viral English-language Reels end with "comment a word", against 10% of viral French ones. US creators use their best lead tool four times less.

There's a catch. These Reels get fewer views: 0.43 million for the middle one, against 1.45 million for the rest. And 9 of the 17 come from accounts that sell content or Instagram coaching. The trick works when the freebie leads to something people will pay for. With no offer behind it, you're collecting comments.

Brand deals: what they pay and when you qualify

Brands pay small accounts. Influencer Marketing Hub puts Instagram rates at $10 to $100 per post for nano creators and $150 to $500 for micro creators in its 2026 guide. One US creator with 22,000 followers in the parenting niche said on Reddit in June 2026 that they charge $500 to $1,000 per video.

Your follower count matters less than you think. Among viral Reels where I know the account size, about 390 of them, 23% come from accounts under 100,000 followers. Brands look at engagement first. Check yours with the engagement rate calculator, then compare it with the rate cards in how much influencers make.

Subscriptions and Gifts: the thresholds

Subscriptions need over 10,000 followers and an 18+ professional account. You pick a monthly price, and subscribers get exclusive content. At launch, TechCrunch listed eight price points from $0.99 to $99.99.

Meta takes no platform fee, but in-app sign-ups go through Apple or Google, which keep roughly 30%, per Influencer Marketing Hub (February 2026). On a $4.99 plan, you keep about $3.49. Payouts land on the 21st of the following month.

Gifts are easier to get and pay far less. A viewer buys stars and sends them on your Reel. You get $0.01 per star, so $100 takes 10,000 stars. The bar was 500 followers in May 2024. Think of Gifts as a tip jar.

The Facebook side door

Meta pays for Reels on Facebook, which it doesn't do on Instagram. In March 2026 it launched Creator Fast Track: $1,000 a month for creators with over 100,000 followers on Instagram, TikTok or YouTube, and $3,000 above 1 million, for three months. You have to post at least 15 Reels on Facebook within 30 days, spread over 10 different days, according to The Next Web (March 18, 2026).

You can reuse Reels you made for Instagram, as long as they're original and haven't been posted on Facebook yet, per Meta's program page. After three months, you keep earning from Facebook's regular monetization on eligible Reels. The page doesn't list eligible countries, so check it from your own account. If you already have 100,000 followers on Instagram, this is the closest thing to a paycheck Meta offers.

FTC rules for sponsored Reels

Any paid deal or free product has to be disclosed. The FTC's guide for influencers says plain words like "ad" or "sponsored" work, while "sp", "spon" and "collab" don't. In a video, the disclosure belongs in the video, not just in the caption. The FTC also warns against relying on a platform's built-in label alone. Add Instagram's paid partnership tag if you like, but say "ad" in the Reel too.

My take

Build an offer before you chase views. The data all points the same way: 98% of viral Reels sell nothing, Instagram doesn't pay per view, and the only Reels that turn attention into leads end with "comment a word", with 10 times the comments. Those leads are worth money only if you have a guide, a service or a product to send them to. My limit: I measure viral Reels, 17 comment-a-word Reels is a small sample, and it doesn't tell me how many comments turn into sales. What to do tomorrow: pick one free resource that leads to your offer, and end your next Reel with the word people should comment to get it.

Frequently asked questions

How many followers do you need to make money on Instagram?

None for brand deals or your own offer. Gifts needed 500 followers as of May 2024, and subscriptions need over 10,000. A nano account can charge $10 to $100 a post, per Influencer Marketing Hub.

Does Instagram pay for Reels in the US?

Only by invite. Bonuses require 5 million views a month for three months, and Instagram publishes no rate. For per-view pay, look at how to make money on TikTok and run your numbers in the TikTok money calculator.

Do you pay taxes on Instagram income?

Yes. Money from Gifts, subscriptions and brand deals is taxable income in the US. Set aside part of every payout and check your case with a tax professional.
