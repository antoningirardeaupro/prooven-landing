How to get more followers on TikTok in 2026: only 4% of viral videos ask for the follow

By Antonin Girardeau, founder of Prooven · Published October 28, 2026 · 9 min read

To get more followers on TikTok, give people a reason to come back: a numbered series, a promised next part, a face they recognize. Asking for the follow barely matters. Of nearly 500 viral videos I analyzed one by one (2023 to July 2026), only 4% end with "follow for more." And bought followers don't stick: TikTok says it removes fake followers it detects (Community Guidelines, effective September 24, 2026).

In short
  • Follower count is not a direct factor in whether TikTok recommends your video (TikTok Newsroom, June 2020). A brand-new account can get views.
  • Buying followers backfires. TikTok strips fake followers, and content that pushes people to inflate follows is kept off the For You feed.
  • Only 4% of the viral videos I analyzed ask for the follow. English-language creators do it least: 3% of their viral videos, against 11% for French ones.
  • Numbered series ("Part 7," "Day 5 of 30") make up 4% of viral videos overall and 8% for accounts under 100,000 followers.
  • The bigger the account, the more its viral videos rely on the person: 32% between 10K and 100K followers, 54% above 1 million.

Followers don't get you views

TikTok ranks the video, not the account. In its official explainer of the For You feed (June 2020), the company wrote that "neither follower count nor whether the account has had previous high-performing videos are direct factors in the recommendation system." The same post says that following an account helps refine what you see next.

So the order runs backwards from what most people assume. Views come first, one video at a time. Followers come later, when a video makes someone want the next one. If your views haven't taken off yet, start with how to go viral on TikTok. If they're stuck at zero, read why your TikTok has 0 views first. You can't convert people who never see you.

What TikTok does with bought followers

It deletes them. TikTok's Community Guidelines, in force since September 24, 2026, put it plainly: "If we detect accounts or content with inauthentic metrics, we'll remove fake likes, followers, or other inflated signals." The rules also ban trading services that artificially boost engagement.

The second trap gets less attention. "Content that tries to manipulate people into giving gifts or inflating likes or follows isn't eligible for the FYF." Follow-for-follow chains fit that description. The video stays up, but strangers stop seeing it.

The third cost shows up in your numbers. A padded account with 10,000 followers and 300 views per video has an engagement rate no brand will touch. Check yours with the engagement rate calculator before you pitch anyone.

Only 1 viral video in 25 says "follow for more"

Viral videos almost never ask for the follow. Across nearly 500 viral videos, about 4% close with a line like "follow for more" or "subscribe for more tips." The same share closes with "comment this word and I'll send you the link." The other 92% do neither.

English-language creators ask for the follow less than anyone in my data: 3% of viral videos in English (over 330 videos) against 11% of viral videos in French (about 70).

Asking doesn't seem to hurt reach. The typical "follow for more" video got 1.7 million views, against 1.4 million for all viral videos. It does get shared less: 8.4 shares per 1,000 views, against 12.1 overall. A follow request reads like an ad, and nobody sends an ad to a friend. That group holds about 20 videos, so treat it as a lean, not a law.

How your ending changes the reaction

The ending of a video mostly decides what kind of reaction you get, not how many views. I flagged every viral video whose structure includes a call-to-action step (follow, comment, like, read the caption). 45% have one. Those videos run longer, 65 seconds against 38, and get fewer views.

Read the table as a menu. Want views? Ending with no ask costs you nothing. Want comments? Ask for one specific word. Want followers? "Follow for more" moves neither comments nor shares, so you need something else.

"Comment a word" gets 10x the comments and a third of the views

Asking for a specific word in the comments multiplies comments by almost 10. Viral videos ending with "comment 'content'" or "just comment GROWTH & I'll send you the link" average 5.1 comments per 1,000 views. The typical viral video gets 0.54.

The catch: the typical comment-a-word video gets 0.43 million views, a third of the overall figure. This ending is built to sell a guide, a course or a DM link. Content strategist Jun Yuh closed an October 2025 Reel with "Comment 'content' and I'll show you how to do this everyday." It got 363,000 views and 2,546 comments. Great for building a list. Not how you reach new people.

A series gives people a reason to follow

A series turns one video into an appointment. When the title says "Part 1," a viewer who liked it knows Part 2 exists, and following becomes the way not to miss it. It's still rare: about 4% of the viral videos I analyzed are numbered ("part," "episode," "day 5 of 30").

Smaller accounts use it twice as often. Under 100,000 followers, 8% of viral videos are series episodes, against 4% above that line. The account Nate - More Sales Memes posted "Part 370" of its sales jokes in February 2025: 9 seconds, 5.3 million views. Dan Koe, with 1.8 million followers, labeled a book list "Part 1" and captioned it "like or comment if you want a part 2."

The smartest example ties the series to the follow itself. A creator called Andy is building a cafe and raises its budget with every new follower, one day per episode:

"Day 2 of increasing my cafe's budget for every follower I get."

Andy, Reel from December 9, 2025, 6.2 million views

Here the follow is part of the story: each viewer moves the project forward. The series group holds about 20 videos, so read these numbers as a direction.

The bigger the account, the more the person matters

Large accounts go viral on personality, small ones on format. For every video, I noted what actually carries it: the person (face, voice, story), the script, or a visual concept that would work with anyone. Then I matched it with the account's follower count.

Between 10K and 100K followers, a third of viral videos rest on the person. Past 1 million, it's more than half. This data can't prove which way the arrow points. The simplest reading still holds up: people follow someone, rarely a format. A format brings you views. Your face, your voice and your recurring opinions bring the same people back.

What to do this week

Here's the order I'd follow, starting from an account that already gets some views.

  1. Pick a format that already worked in your niche and number it: "Part 1" in the hook and in the caption.
  2. Post Part 2 within 48 hours, with the same opening line and framing, so people spot the series in their feed.
  3. Put your face or your voice in at least one step of every video, even when the format carries it.
  4. Swap "follow for more" for a specific promise: "Part 3 is the best one."
  5. Open the analytics of each video and compare followers gained, not just views. Keep the format that brings the most.

Followers start to matter for money once you have them. The Creator Rewards Program in the US requires 10,000 followers and 100,000 views in the last 30 days (TikTok Creator Academy, September 2026). The thresholds are broken down in how many followers you need to get paid on TikTok, and the TikTok money calculator turns your views into dollars.

My take

Stop asking for the follow and start promising a sequel. My data shows viral videos almost never ask (4%), and the ones that do earn no extra comments or shares. What brings people back is a Part 2 they want to see and a person they recognize. Small accounts seem to get this already: they run series twice as often as big ones. The limit of my view is clear. I only analyzed videos that worked, mostly Instagram Reels, and I can't see how many followers each one brought in. I measure what viral videos do, not their exact effect on the follower count.

Common questions

How long does it take to get 1,000 followers on TikTok?

TikTok publishes no figure, and my data doesn't measure it. It depends on views: one video at 500,000 views can get you there, while a hundred videos at 200 views won't.

Does follow for follow work?

It grows a number, not an audience. TikTok's rules keep content that pushes people to inflate follows off the For You feed, and those followers don't watch your videos anyway.

Should I still say "follow for more" at the end?

In my data it costs no views: 1.7 million for the typical video, across about 20 videos. It also adds no shares or comments. A promised next part does the same job with a real reason.

Do followers matter to the algorithm?

Not directly. TikTok judges each video on how the people who see it react. I break down what it measures in how the TikTok algorithm works.
