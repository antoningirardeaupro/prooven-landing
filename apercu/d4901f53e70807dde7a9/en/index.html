YouTube Shorts algorithm 2026: 15-second Shorts get 2.2x the views

By Antonin Girardeau, founder of Prooven · Published November 2, 2026 · 10 min read

The YouTube Shorts algorithm pushes the Shorts people choose to watch instead of swiping past. YouTube's Shorts product lead said so in August 2023: a view should capture your "intent of watching," and since March 31, 2025 that measure is called "engaged views." In the vertical viral videos I analyzed, the shortest travel furthest: at 15 seconds or less, half pass 2.6 million views, 2.2 times more than videos between 16 and 60 seconds.

In short
  • Since March 31, 2025, YouTube counts a Shorts view the moment it starts playing. Payouts still use the stricter number, engaged views.
  • Shorts can run 3 minutes since October 15, 2024. Past one minute, a single active Content ID claim blocks the Short worldwide.
  • Viral videos of 15 seconds or less get 2.6 million views for the typical video, against 1.2 million between 16 and 60 seconds.
  • Videos over one minute get twice the shares per view: 14.1 per 1,000 views, against 6.8 for the very short ones.

What YouTube has said about the Shorts algorithm

YouTube runs Shorts on different rules from long-form. Todd Sherman, then Shorts product lead, explained it on the Creator Insider channel in August 2023. On long-form, you pick a video on purpose. On Shorts, "people are swiping through a feed, and discovering things as they go." So the system reads different signals.

Sherman gave three plain rules. Posting more does not change your reach by itself, but a pile of weak videos with "meager engagement" works against you. On deleting a Short to re-upload it, he was blunt: "I would not advise that," because the system may flag it as spam. Hashtags help when they tie the video to a real event or a clear topic.

The stakes are big. On June 18, 2025, at Cannes Lions, YouTube CEO Neal Mohan said Shorts average more than 200 billion daily views. He announced it less than three months after the view count change described below.

The signal that matters: watched or swiped away

For every Short, YouTube wants to know one thing: did this person watch it on purpose, or swipe past? Sherman put it this way in 2023: "What we try and do with a view is have it encode for your intent of watching that thing." It doesn't have to be your favorite video. You just chose to watch it.

Then the public counter changed. Since March 31, 2025, a view counts every time a Short starts or replays, with no minimum watch time. The old measure stayed, renamed "engaged views" in YouTube Analytics. YouTube uses it for Partner Program eligibility and for revenue sharing.

So the big number under your Short went up, and it stopped telling you whether people stayed. To check your hook, open YouTube Studio and look at the share of viewers who watched your Short instead of swiping away. YouTube publishes no official target for that rate. The "aim for 70%" rule you see on tool blogs has no source behind it.

Length: the shortest Shorts travel furthest

In my data, videos of 15 seconds or less get twice the views of every other length. I sorted nearly 500 vertical viral videos by length. Half of those at 15 seconds or less pass 2.6 million views. Between 16 and 60 seconds, the typical video gets 1.2 million.

The floor moves even more. Among videos of 15 seconds or less, three in four pass one million views. Between 16 and 60 seconds, one in four stays under 269,000. A very short video almost always gets finished, and it loops before the thumb moves. On Shorts, each loop has counted as a view since March 2025.

None of the nearly 500 viral videos I analyzed runs past 3 minutes, the Shorts limit. But 43% run longer than one minute, and on YouTube every one of them is exposed to the Content ID block.

Longer Shorts get shared more

Short videos win views; longer ones win shares. Between 61 seconds and 3 minutes, the typical viral video gets 14.1 shares per 1,000 views. At 15 seconds or less, it gets 6.8.

It comes down to what the video is. Two thirds of the viral videos over one minute teach something, and 64% get saved as a "useful resource." Under 15 seconds, that share drops to 31%. You send a tutorial to a friend. A 7-second joke, you watch three times and keep scrolling.

Cleo Abram, who built her audience on YouTube, shows the long side. Her 68-second demo of a new LEGO Smart Brick, posted March 6, 2026, reached 6.1 million views. Davide De Pierro went further with 91 seconds of generational nostalgia: 21.1 million views on July 17, 2025, opening on one line:

"Thank God when I was young the trend was to get home at 6am instead of going to Pilates every morning with matcha."

Davide De Pierro, Reel from July 17, 2025

The middle group, 16 to 60 seconds, is the riskiest. It has the lowest floor: one video in four stays under 269,000 views. Too long to loop, too short to teach something worth forwarding.

Formats that work under 15 seconds

Under 15 seconds, almost half the videos follow one structure: a setup, then a punchline. Comedy makes up 34% of formats, against 12% between 16 and 60 seconds. And 73% of these videos can be remade as is, with no acting skill and no gear.

The sharpest case lasts one second. Kien Nguyen, a content creator, posted a static night shot with one line of on-screen text on August 22, 2024. It reached 83.8 million views:

"POV: Me editing a reel till 3 AM, only to get 69 views."

Kien Nguyen, Reel from August 22, 2024

Everything sits in the text. You read it, you laugh, and the video has already looped. Soph did better in 13 seconds: a parent and child talking in the car, 137.8 million views on December 13, 2025.

The hook: one second before the swipe

On Shorts, you get one second. That's Jenny Hoyos, the American Shorts creator, on YouTube's official blog on January 28, 2025: "I really do think you have one second to hook someone, especially on Shorts." Her hook has three parts: surprise the viewer, build curiosity, then pay it off.

My data agrees. In 78% of viral videos over one minute, the hook opens a question that only the ending closes. Under 15 seconds, it's 55%, because the punchline arrives before curiosity has time to build. Cleo Abram's first words, "This incredible new LEGO Smart Brick," promise a reveal you have to stay for.

Thumbnails barely matter for discovery. On the same blog, Hoyos and Sherman say "99.9% of the views are coming from the Shorts feed." Nobody sees your thumbnail before the Short plays.

The trap with Shorts over one minute

A Short longer than one minute with an active Content ID claim is blocked globally on YouTube. YouTube's help center says so, whatever the claim type. The Short is then neither playable nor recommended.

The typical case: you repost a 90-second TikTok with a trending sound. On YouTube, that track often belongs to a label that protects it. You don't get a copyright strike, but the video disappears. Remove the music, use a track from the YouTube Audio Library, or cut under one minute.

The pay rules moved too. From February 1, 2027, you need 10 million qualified Shorts views over the last 90 days to earn your share of Shorts revenue. To compare what the same views pay on TikTok, the TikTok money calculator does the math.

My take

Pick a side before you film: 15 seconds for views, over a minute for shares. The middle, 16 to 60 seconds, has the lowest floor in my data, with one video in four under 269,000 views. If you do comedy or POV, cut under 15 seconds and put the whole joke in on-screen text. If you teach, commit to 90 seconds and open on a concrete promise, the way Cleo Abram does. Either way, no claimed trending sound past the one-minute mark. The limit: my sample holds no YouTube Shorts, mostly Instagram Reels. I can show what makes a vertical video travel, not the exact weight YouTube gives each signal. To see what counts as viral for your account size, the thresholds are in how many views is viral.

Frequently asked questions

Do Shorts help my long-form channel?

YouTube publishes no numbers on it. Sherman only said in 2023 that Shorts and long-form have different viewing habits and run on different rules. Nothing shows that a hit Short lifts your long videos.

Should I post a Short every day?

Not if quality drops. Sherman said that energy "is probably better spent in just making a better video and fewer of them." The playbook for growing with fewer videos is in the guide on how to go viral on TikTok, and it carries over to Shorts.

How do I know if my Short was really watched?

Check engaged views in YouTube Analytics, not the public counter. Then the engagement rate calculator tells you whether likes and shares keep up.
