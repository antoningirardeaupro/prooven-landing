TikTok hooks: what makes a hook work, from 500 viral videos (drop-in scenes get 2 million views)

By Antonin Girardeau, founder of Prooven · Published November 23, 2026 · 11 min read

A TikTok hook works when its first sentence promises something the viewer only gets by staying. I analyzed about 500 viral videos one by one (2023 to July 2026). The most common hook is a contrarian claim, used by 20% of them. The hook that pulls the most views is the drop-in scene: 2.0 million views for the typical video, against 1.4 million overall. TikTok tells advertisers to land the hook within the first 6 seconds (TikTok Ads Help, June 2025).

In short
  • I sorted the opening line of every viral video into 7 hook types. The contrarian claim, a line that says the viewer is wrong, leads with 20% of videos.
  • The drop-in scene, which puts the viewer inside a situation from the first word, gets the most views (2.0 million). It also gets the fewest comments.
  • Opening with your own story is the weakest type: 1.1 million views and half the shares of a contrarian claim.
  • The typical spoken hook in a viral video is 10 words long. The on-screen text version is 6 words.
  • TikTok's own ads guidance: state your point in the first 3 seconds and put your hook in the first 6 (June 2025).

What a hook has to do on TikTok

A hook has one job: stop the thumb before it swipes. Every platform judges your video first on whether people stay. TikTok said it in its official For You explainer of June 18, 2020: a user finishing a longer video from start to end counts more than a weak signal like a single view. Instagram says the same about Reels. One of its most important predictions is how likely you are to watch a reel "all the way through" (Instagram, May 31, 2023).

YouTube hands you the number. In YouTube Studio, "Stayed to watch" shows the percentage of times viewers kept watching past the first seconds of your Short. That figure is your hook score, and only you can see it. On TikTok, the retention chart in TikTok Studio tells you the same thing: the exact second people leave.

The 7 hook types behind viral videos

Almost every viral hook fits one of 7 families. I broke each video into its steps and tagged the technique used in the first one. Here they are, from most to least common, with a real hook for each.

Which hook type gets the most views

The drop-in scene wins on views: 2.0 million for the typical video in that group. Commands come next at 1.8 million, then contrarian claims at 1.5 million. For reference, the typical viral video across all hook types gets 1.4 million views.

Statistic hooks get the fewest views, 0.6 million. I only found 18 of them, so treat that figure with care. They also get twice the usual number of comments (1.1 per 1,000 views, against 0.55). A number gives people something to argue with.

Contrarian hooks get shared twice as much as confessions

Contrarian claims get 15 shares per 1,000 views. Confessions get 7.5. Shares matter: Instagram lists the chance you reshare a reel first among its key Reels predictions (May 2023).

A contrarian claim works because it is about the viewer. They want to know if they are wrong. If they are right, they send it to the friend who makes the same mistake. Adam Cam got 6.1 million views on a 59-second reel built this way:

"Listen, I'm all for a bit of workplace banter, but what she did was out of line."

Adam Cam, reel posted June 17, 2026

He takes a side in the first sentence, before you even know the story. The template travels to any niche: "I'm all for [thing your audience likes], but [specific behavior] is out of line." The catch is the payoff. A contrarian claim with nothing solid behind it is clickbait, and people leave before the end.

Drop-in scenes get the most views and the fewest comments

A hook that sets a scene gets 2.0 million views and half the usual comment rate (0.28 per 1,000 views). The viewer is inside the story before deciding to stay. They watch to the end, then swipe on without typing anything.

Taryn Arnold Scott hit 23.9 million views with a 27-second reel posted July 20, 2025. The hook makes you picture someone before you know why:

"If someone pops in your head when I say the thing that I'm about to say, I think that that's a sign that you should send this to them."

taryn arnold scott, reel posted July 20, 2025

The word "if" does the work. It makes your brain play the scene. Use the drop-in scene when you want reach. When you want comments, pick a question or a number.

Starting with yourself costs you viewers

Confession is the weakest hook type in my data: 1.1 million views and 7.5 shares per 1,000 views, the lowest of the seven. It is also the default for most new creators: "So today I want to talk about…", "When I first started…". The viewer does not know you yet. Your life only matters to them if it says something about theirs.

Hooks where the creator talks about themselves get half the shares of contrarian hooks: 7.5 per 1,000 views, against 15.0.

The confessions that work turn the camera back on the viewer fast. Davide De Pierro got 6.1 million views on March 18, 2026 with "No one warns you that getting older mostly means deciding what to make with ground beef and chicken for the rest of your life." The line is about him, yet every adult watching sees their own fridge in it.

How long should a TikTok hook be

The typical spoken hook in a viral video is 10 words. Half of all spoken hooks fall between 6 and 16 words. On-screen text is shorter, 6 words for the typical video. Among the videos where I logged the hook word for word, 72% use both: the spoken line plus a short written version on screen.

Word count matters less than the promise. Very short hooks (5 words or fewer) get no extra views: 1.2 million, against 1.4 million for hooks of 16 to 25 words. Those longer hooks also get the most shares (14.9 per 1,000 views). A precise 20-word sentence does more than an empty "Watch this."

On-screen text does matter. In its ads guide, TikTok reports that 40% of the ads with the highest view-through rate use text overlays. So write the 3 to 6 words that carry your promise, large, on the first frame. Viewers scrolling with the sound off will read them.

What the first line has to trigger

In 55% of viral videos, the first step runs on curiosity: it opens a question that only the rest of the video closes. Far behind come belonging to a group (10%, as in "if you're between 35 and 42"), an everyday pain (7%) and a concrete desire (5%).

The hook type is the shape. Curiosity is the engine. A contrarian claim, a question or a command all work if they leave a gap the viewer wants filled. Mr. High Level got 56.2 million views with a 12-word command posted July 4, 2025: "Do this every single day before going to bed, and this happens." The line says neither what to do nor what happens. To find out, you stay for all 23 seconds.

That watch time is also money. On TikTok, only views longer than 5 seconds count toward payouts, as I explain in my guide to the Creator Rewards Program. A hook that holds people raises your views and what they pay.

How to write your hook tomorrow morning

Write three hooks of different types for the same video, then keep the one with the most precise promise. Here are three templates taken from real viral videos. Fill them in with your topic:

  • Contrarian claim: "If your [thing everyone makes] looks like this, you're doing it wrong."
  • Command: "Do this every day before [time of day] and watch what happens."
  • Statistic: "[Long time] of [topic] in [short time]." (modeled on "3 years of therapy in 1 minute," 40.2 million views).

Shoot the hook as your very first shot, with no "hey guys" and no intro. Put it on screen in 6 words or fewer. Post, then open the retention chart in TikTok Studio after 48 hours. My take

Open by putting the viewer in a scene or by telling them they are wrong. Never open with yourself. The data is clear on this: drop-in scenes get 2.0 million views, and contrarian claims get shared twice as often as confessions. If you keep one rule, keep this one: the first word of your video should be about the viewer or their problem. The limit of my take: I only analyzed videos that worked, mostly Instagram Reels. I cannot see the thousands of contrarian hooks that got 200 views because the payoff was weak. A strong hook gets people in the door. The structure behind it keeps them there, which I cover in my guide on how to go viral on TikTok.

Frequently asked questions

How many seconds is a TikTok hook?

TikTok advises putting your hook in the first 6 seconds and your main point in the first 3 (TikTok Ads Help, June 2025). Its ads guide adds that over 63% of the ads with the highest click-through rate show their key message within the first 3 seconds. Aim for a hook you can finish before the 3-second mark.

Do TikTok hooks work on Instagram Reels and YouTube Shorts?

Yes. The videos I analyzed are mostly Instagram Reels, and all three platforms track whether people stay past the opening seconds. Only the label in your analytics changes: retention on TikTok, "Stayed to watch" on YouTube.

Should a hook ask people to follow?

No. Asking for a follow in your first sentence promises the viewer nothing. None of the viral hooks in this article mentions following. They promise information, a result or a scene.

Is it better to say the hook or write it on screen?

Do both. 72% of the viral videos where I logged the hook word for word say it out loud and also show a short version as text. Videos with written hooks only were mostly short visual clips, around 13 seconds.
